- **Q:** What is the difference between static and dynamic compliance? Why is dynamic always lower (2 reasons)?

when is the difference more pronounced?
  **A:** Static compliance: ΔV/ΔP measured at zero flow — breath hold allows all lung units time to fully equilibrate
Dynamic compliance: ΔV/ΔP measured during active breathing between points of zero flow

Dynamic is always lower for two reasons:
1. Airway resistance — during flow, pressure is spent overcoming resistance, not distending lung → apparent compliance falls
2. Unequal time constants — slow units haven't finished filling/emptying at the measurement points → volume change is underestimated for the pressure applied

Frequency dependence: as RR increases, slow units contribute even less volume → dynamic compliance falls further. Widening gap between static and dynamic compliance with increasing RR = hallmark of heterogeneous small airways disease
- **Q:** How is static compliance measured in a spontaneously breathing patient and a ventilated patient? (what is normal ventilated value)

Patient inspires known volumes in steps, holds at each (zero flow → mouth pressure = alveolar pressure)
Oesophageal balloon measures pleural pressure (surrogate)
Plot ΔV vs Δ(alveolar − pleural pressure) → slope = static compliance

Ventilated:
Inspiratory hold → flow = 0 → plateau pressure = alveolar pressure
\[ C = \frac{V_T}{P_{plateau} - PEEP} \]
Normal ventilated value ~60–80 ml/cmH₂O

Note: Cannot use mouth pressure during spontaneous breathing without breath hold — flow means mouth pressure ≠ alveolar pressure

### ✅ F3.v `covered` · 3/~3 cards
*Explain the concept of time constants.*

- **Q:** Define time constant (τ) in respiratory physiology. Give the equation, normal value, and what 1, 3 and 5 τ represent.
  **A:** Definition: Time for an exponential process to reach 63% of its final value. Equivalently — time to complete if initial rate were maintained.

\[ \tau = R \times C \]

Normal alveolar τ ≈ 0.2 seconds (R = 2 cmH₂O/L/s, C = 0.1 L/cmH₂O)

Proportions:
1τ → 63% complete
3τ → 95% complete (practical complete)
5τ → 99% complete

Implication: Normal inspiration needs ≥0.6s (3τ) for adequate alveolar filling
- **Q:** How do high airway resistance and low compliance affect time constants? What are the ventilator implications of each?
  **A:** τ=R×C

High resistance (e.g. asthma):
↑R → ↑τ → slow filling and emptying
Ventilator: ↓RR, prolonged expiratory time (I:E 1:3 or 1:4) to prevent auto-PEEP and dynamic hyperinflation

Low compliance (e.g. ARDS):
↓C → ↓τ → fast filling but to low volume
Lungs tolerate higher RR without gas trapping
Primary concern is driving pressure and volutrauma, not expiratory time

Key distinction: Resistance determines rate of filling; compliance determines final volume achieved
- **Q:** What is pendelluft? When does it occur and why is it harmful in ARDS?
  **A:** Definition: Gas flowing between lung units laterally (alveolus to alveolus) rather than in and out of the airway

Mechanism: Heterogeneous time constants → fast units finish filling before slow units. At inspiration-expiration transition, fast units begin emptying while slow units still filling → gas shifts fast → slow

Harm in ARDS:
Fast units: empty further than intended → cyclic collapse below closing capacity → atelectotrauma
Slow units: receive extra gas beyond set tidal volume → volutrauma/overdistension
Both occur simultaneously from the same breath

Mitigation: PEEP prevents end-expiratory collapse of fast units, reducing the pendelluft pressure gradient

- **Q:** Surfactant: explain its mechanism of action and why it causes hysteresis in the PV curve
  **A:** Problem: LaPlace's Law — P = 2T/r
Without surfactant, smaller alveoli require greater pressure to remain open than larger ones. At any given distending pressure, smaller alveoli are inherently less stable — once they begin to collapse, r falls, required pressure rises further, collapse becomes self-reinforcing. Gas displaced into larger alveoli.

Mechanism:
Surfactant reduces surface tension proportionally with smaller surface area
- Alveolus shrinks → DPPC molecules pack more densely → T falls → required opening pressure doesn't rise as LaPlace predicts
- Stabilises alveoli of different sizes by abolishing the instability gradient

Hysteresis:
- Inflation: surfactant disordered, not yet organised at interface → higher pressure needed to recruit and open alveoli
- Deflation: molecules already organised and densely packed → T falls near zero → alveolus stays open at much lower pressure than needed to open it
- Therefore: easier to keep open than to open
- Deflation limb sits left of inflation limb on PV curve

Proven by saline-filled lung experiment — eliminating the air-water interface abolishes most hysteresis, confirming surfactant rather than tissue viscoelasticity is the dominant cause.

Also prevents pulmonary oedema:
Surface tension at the curved alveolar surface reduces hydrostatic pressure in the pericapillary interstitium → sucks fluid out of capillaries into alveoli. Surfactant reduces surface tension → abolishes this driving force → keeps alveoli dry.

- **Q:** Why is dissolved O₂ inadequate as the sole oxygen transport mechanism? When does it become clinically critical?
  **A:** The numbers:
Dissolved O₂ = 0.003 × PaO₂ = 0.3 mL/100mL at PaO₂ 100 mmHg
At CO 5 L/min → delivers only ~15 mL O₂/min dissolved
Resting O₂ consumption ~250 mL/min → incompatible with life without Hb

When dissolved O₂ becomes critical — CO poisoning:
COHb occupies binding sites AND left-shifts OHDC for remaining Hb → Hb-bound O₂ unavailable for loading AND unloading. Dissolved O₂ is the only functional carrier.
Treatment: 100% O₂ → maximises dissolved O₂ AND competitively displaces CO (half-life ~5hrs on air → ~60–90 mins on 100% O₂)
- **Q:** Explain the structure of Hb structure, and cooperative binding. And how this causes OHDC shape
  **A:** Hb structure:
globular protein Tetramer — 2α + 2β chains, each with a haem group containing Fe²⁺
Two conformational states:
— T-state (tense) = deoxygenated, low O₂ affinity
— R-state (relaxed) = oxygenated, high O₂ affinity

Cooperative binding:
Each O₂ that binds shifts the molecule toward R-state → each subsequent binding site has progressively higher affinity → binding accelerates as sites fill

Sigmoid shape:
— Lower steep portion: cooperative binding accelerating — small ↑PO₂ → large ↑saturation. Small drop in tissue PO₂ releases large amounts of O₂
— Upper flat portion: binding sites nearly saturated — large ↑PO₂ → minimal ↑saturation. Protects loading against moderate drops in PAO₂
- **Q:** Oxygen delivery (DO₂): 
equation? 
normal value?
 units?
  **A:** DO₂ = CaO₂ × CO × 10
— CaO₂ in mL/100mL, CO in L/min, ×10 converts dL→L
— Units: mL/min
= 20 x 5 x 10 = 1000ml/min
- **Q:** Define the Bohr effect and the Haldane effect. How do they interact physiologically?
  **A:** Bohr effect:
↑CO₂ and ↑H⁺ at tissues → ↓Hb affinity for O₂ → right shift of OHDC → ↑O₂ unloading
"High CO₂ and H⁺ makes Hb like O₂ less"

Haldane effect:
Deoxygenation of Hb → ↑ability to carry CO₂ — both as carbaminoHb AND by buffering H⁺
"O₂ unloading makes Hb like CO₂ and H⁺ more"

Physiological coupling at tissues:
↑Tissue CO₂/H⁺ → Bohr → O₂ unloads → deoxyHb → Haldane → CO₂ loads
Each process facilitates the other

Key distinction: Bohr = CO₂ drives O₂ off. Haldane = O₂ leaving drives CO₂ on.

Components:
• 1.34 = Hüfner's constant (mL O₂/g Hb)
• Hb in g/dL, SaO₂ as decimal, PaO₂ in mmHg
• Result in mL/100mL blood

(1.34 × 15 × 0.975) + (0.003 × 100) = 19.6 + 0.3 ≈ 20 mL/100mL

### ✅ F8.ii `covered` · 3 cards
*Explain the oxyhaemoglobin dissociation curve and factors that may alter it.*

- **Q:** Describe the oxyhaemoglobin dissociation curve — what it plots, why it has its shape, and define P50- and give normal value
  **A:** What it plots:
SaO2 (y-axis) vs PaO2 (x-axis) — sigmoidal relationship

Why sigmoid — two separate mechanisms:

Lower steep portion:
Cooperative binding — each O2 that binds causes a conformational shift (T-state → R-state), making the next binding site progressively easier. Binding accelerates as more sites fill.

Upper flat portion:
Saturation — binding sites nearly all occupied. Large increases in PaO2 recruit only the tiny fraction of remaining unbound sites. Marginal gain per unit pressure becomes negligible.

P50:
PaO2 at which Hb is 50% saturated. Normal = 3.5 kPa (26–27 mmHg)
- Right shift → ↑ P50 → ↓ affinity → ↑ O2 unloading
- Left shift → ↓ P50 → ↑ affinity → ↓ O2 unloading

Clinical significance of sigmoid shape:
- Flat upper portion: protects against desaturation with moderate drops in PaO2
- Steep lower (technically middle) portion: efficient O2 unloading at tissues — small drop in tissue PO2 releases large amounts of O2
- **Q:** What causes right and left shifts of the OHDC? What is the mnemonic for right shift?
  **A:** Right shift (↑ P50, ↓ affinity, ↑ O2 unloading to tissues):
- ↑ Temperature
- ↑ PaCO2
- ↓ pH
- ↑ 2,3-DPG (chronic anaemia, altitude, chronic hypoxia)

Left shift (↓ P50, ↑ affinity, ↓ O2 unloading):
- ↓ Temperature
- ↓ PaCO2
- ↑ pH
- ↓ 2,3-DPG
- Foetal Hb (HbF) — lacks β chains, 2,3-DPG binds poorly
- Carboxyhaemoglobin (CO)
- Methaemoglobin

Mnemonic for right shift:
CADET, face right
CO2 · Acid · 2,3-DPG · Exercise · Temperature

Physiological logic:
Active tissues generate CO2, heat and acid → right shift exactly where O2 is needed most. In pulmonary capillaries, CO2 expelled → pH rises → left shift → efficient O2 loading. (Bohr effect)
- **Q:** Why is carbon monoxide poisoning so dangerous? Include the effect on the OHDC and the pulse oximetry trap.
  **A:** Double hit mechanism:

1. CO has ~250x the affinity of O2 for haemoglobin → occupies binding sites → reduces O2 carrying capacity
2. CO binding left-shifts the OHDC for remaining haem sites → what O2 is carried is not released to tissues

Net effect: dramatically reduced O2 delivery AND reduced O2 unloading — tissue hypoxia even if some Hb is still oxygenated

Pulse oximetry trap:
SpO2 reads falsely normal — pulse oximetry uses only two wavelengths and cannot distinguish oxyhaemoglobin from carboxyhaemoglobin. Patient may appear well-saturated while profoundly hypoxic.
Diagnosis requires co-oximetry (ABG) which measures COHb directly.

Treatment:
100% O2 — competitively displaces CO, reduces COHb half-life from ~5 hours (room air) to ~60–90 minutes

### ✅ F8.iii `covered` · 3/~4 cards
*Describe the carriage of carbon dioxide in blood.*

- **Q:** CO₂ is carried in blood in three forms. What are they and what are their proportions in arterial blood?
  **A:** Three forms:

1. Dissolved CO₂ — obeys Henry's Law. CO₂ is ~20× more soluble than O₂ (0.067 vs 0.003 mL/dL/mmHg).

2. Bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) — formed inside RBC via carbonic anhydrase. Dominant form.

3. Carbamino compounds — CO₂ binds terminal amine groups on globin chains of Hb (not haem). No enzyme required. DeoxyHb binds more CO₂ this way than oxyHb.

Arterial stock proportions:
— HCO₃⁻: 90%
— Dissolved: 5%
— Carbamino: 5%
- **Q:** How do the proportions of CO₂ transport forms differ between arterial stock and the venous-arterial difference? Why the difference?
  **A:** Venous-arterial difference proportions:
— HCO₃⁻: 60%
— Carbamino: 30%
— Dissolved: 10%

Mnemonic: dissolved doubles (5→10%), carbamino ×6 (5→30%), HCO₃⁻ drops (90→60%)

Why carbamino punches above its weight:
Carbamino binding is exquisitely sensitive to oxygenation state — deoxyHb binds CO₂ as carbamino far more avidly than oxyHb (Haldane effect)
At tissues: O₂ unloads → deoxyHb → rapid ↑carbamino binding
At lung: O₂ loads → oxyHb → carbamino releases CO₂
5% of standing pool, 30% of CO₂ flux — sixfold overrepresentation
- **Q:** How is bicarbonate formed during CO₂ carriage? Why does this occur in the RBC, what happens to waste products, what is the chloride shift?
  **A:** Reaction (carbonic anhydrase catalyses first step):
\[CO_2 + H_2O \rightarrow H_2CO_3 \rightarrow H^+ + HCO_3^-\]

Why in the RBC:
Carbonic anhydrase (CA) located inside RBC — accelerates the first reaction ~5000-fold. Too slow in plasma.

Chloride shift (Hamburger shift):
HCO₃⁻ accumulates inside RBC → diffuses out into plasma
Membrane impermeable to cations → Cl⁻ shifts into RBC to maintain electrical neutrality

H⁺ disposal:
H⁺ buffered by deoxyHb inside RBC — deoxyHb is a better proton acceptor than oxyHb → limits pH change in venous blood

### ✅ G1.iii `covered` · 9 cards
*Describe the structure and functional significance of the excitatory, conductive, and contractile elements of the heart.*

- **Q:** How does cardiac muscle function as a single unit despite being composed of individual cells? What structures enable this, and what are their distinct roles?
  **A:** Individual cardiomyocytes connected end-to-end by intercalated discs, containing:

Gap junctions → low-resistance electrical channels → AP propagates cell-to-cell → entire ventricle depolarises as one

Desmosomes + fascia adherens → mechanical anchors → contractile force transmitted across junction

Cells branch → 3D mesh → not a true syncytium (each cell has own nucleus) but functions as one
- **Q:** Describe the structural hierarchy of cardiac muscle from sarcomere to myocardium, including approximate dimensions.
  **A:** Sarcomere (~2 µm) — fundamental contractile unit between two Z-lines. Contains thick filaments (myosin, A band) and thin filaments (actin, I band) in overlapping arrangement

Myofibril — thousands of sarcomeres in series → shortening summates along length

Cardiomyocyte (~100 µm long, 20 µm wide) — hundreds of myofibrils in parallel → force summates across width. ~30% of cell volume is mitochondria — reflects near-exclusively oxidative metabolism

Myocardium — cardiomyocytes connect end-to-end via intercalated discs and branch → three-dimensional mesh → ventricle wrings and twists rather than shortening linearly

One nucleus per cell — functional but not true syncytium
- **Q:** Troponin has 3 subunits. Name each, and its function/binding
  **A:** Troponin-C: binds Ca²⁺ → conformational change → tropomyosin shifts → exposes myosin binding sites on actin → cross-bridge formation

Troponin-I: inhibitory — acts on Actin, keeps tropomyosin blocking the myosin binding site on actin at rest. PKA phosphorylation → ↓Ca²⁺ affinity of TnC → faster Ca²⁺ release → lusitropy

Troponin-T: anchors troponin complex to tropomyosin

One troponin complex per 7 actin monomers
- **Q:** Describe excitation-contraction coupling in cardiac muscle. Why is cardiac muscle uniquely dependent on extracellular calcium?
  **A:** AP → T-tubule depolarisation → L-type Ca²⁺ channels (DHPR) open → small Ca²⁺ entry

Ca²⁺ binds ryanodine receptors on SR → large SR Ca²⁺ release (CICR). Intracellular Ca²⁺ ↑ from 0.1 → 100 µmol/L. Amplification ratio ~1:4–5

↑Ca²⁺ binds troponin-C → tropomyosin shifts → cross-bridge cycling → contraction

Contrast skeletal muscle: DHPR mechanically coupled directly to RyR — no Ca²⁺ entry needed. Cardiac DHPR is an L-type channel — remove extracellular Ca²⁺ → no trigger → no CICR → no contraction
- **Q:** Describe the 5 steps of the myosin cross-bridge cycle. Where does ATP act, and what is rigor?
  **A:** 1. Cocked state — myosin head hydrolyses ATP → ADP + Pi retained. Head in high-energy configuration, not yet bound to actin
2. Binding — tropomyosin shifts (Ca²⁺ via TnC) → myosin head binds exposed actin site
3. Power stroke — Pi released → myosin head pivots → actin pulled toward centre of sarcomere → ADP released
4. Rigor state — myosin remains tightly bound to actin. No nucleotide present — this is rigor mortis (ATP depleted, cross-bridges locked)
5. Detachment + re-cocking — new ATP binds myosin → releases from actin → ATP hydrolysed → head returns to high-energy position

ATP has two functions: (ATP binds binding = actin and myosin detachment) and (Hydrolysis = myosin head re-cocking). Force generated at power stroke (step 3)
- **Q:** How does the cardiomyocyte remove cytosolic calcium to achieve relaxation? Name the 2 main and 2 minor mechanisms.
  **A:** SERCA (~80%, dominant) — ↑Ca²⁺ pumped back into SR. ATP-dependent. Phospholamban inhibits at rest → PKA phosphorylation → dissociates from SERCA → disinhibited → ↑Ca²⁺ reuptake → lusitropy

NCX (~20%) — forward mode: 3Na⁺ in, 1Ca²⁺ out. Driven by Na⁺ gradient. ↓Na⁺ gradient (e.g. digoxin) → ↓Ca²⁺ extrusion → ↑intracellular Ca²⁺

Minor: sarcolemmal Ca²⁺-ATPase and mitochondrial uptake — not clinically significant
- **Q:** What is the mechanism by which digoxin increases myocardial contractility?
  **A:** Digoxin inhibits Na/K-ATPase → ↑intracellular Na⁺ → ↓Na⁺ gradient → NCX forward mode impaired → ↓Ca²⁺ extruded → ↑intracellular Ca²⁺ → ↑SR Ca²⁺ loading → stronger CICR → ↑contractility

(Same Ca²⁺ overload mechanism underlies digoxin toxicity DADs)
- **Q:** Why can cardiac muscle not be tetanised?
  **A:** ARP (~200ms) spans almost entire contraction (~250ms AP) → cell inexcitable until already relaxing → summation impossible

Caused by prolonged phase 2 plateau (ICa(L)) → fast Na⁺ channels remain inactivated

Contrast skeletal muscle: AP 1–2ms, contraction ~100ms → excitable throughout → summation → tetanus

Consequence: tetanic cardiac contraction → no diastole → no filling → fatal
- **Q:** What is the cardiac fibrous skeleton, what does it do electrically and mechanically, and what is the clinical consequence of a breach?
  **A:** Rings of type I collagen encircling all four valve orifices — complete electrical and mechanical barrier between atria and ventricles

Electrical: only normal conduction pathway across it is AV node → Bundle of His

Mechanical: structural anchorage for valve leaflets + supports ventricular wall during systole

Breach — accessory pathway (WPW): bypasses AV node → ↓PR interval, delta wave, fusion QRS → macro-reentrant SVT → AF via accessory pathway → ↑ventricular rate → VF risk

Desmosome failure — ARVC (Arrhythmogenic Right Ventricular Cardiomyopathy (ARVC) : mutations in desmosomal proteins → myocyte death → fibrofatty replacement → re-entry → sudden cardiac death

### ✅ G2.ii `covered` · 3 cards

- **Q:** Cardiac conduction pathway: sequence and conduction velocity at each step?
  **A:** SA node → Atrial muscle 1 m/s (+ Bachmann's bundle to left atrium) → AV node 0.05 m/s (0.1s delay) → Bundle of His 1-2 m/s → RBB/LBB 1-2 m/s → Purkinje fibres 1-4 m/s → Ventricular muscle 0.5 m/s

Slowest — AV node (0.05 m/s): Slow-response tissue, ICa(L) driven, small cell diameter, high internal resistance
Fastest — Purkinje (1-4 m/s): Fast-response, INa driven, largest diameter cells in heart → near-simultaneous ventricular endocardial activation → coordinated contraction
- **Q:** Pacemaker hierarchy: intrinsic rates, overdrive suppression mechanism, and escape rhythms?
  **A:** Intrinsic rates:
SA node: 100-110/min
AV node: 40-60/min
Bundle of His: 40/min
Purkinje fibres: 15-30/min

Overdrive suppression: Faster pacemaker drives lower ones above their intrinsic rate. Na+ entry accelerates. Na/K-ATPase works harder (ATP-dependent, electrogenic: 3Na+ out, 2K+ in). When dominant pacemaker fails, ATPase transiently continues hyperpolarising the escape pacemaker. Pause is longer than expected even for the escape pacemaker's intrinsic rate. Escape rhythm emerges once hyperpolarisation wears off.

Escape rhythms:
AV node takeover — narrow complex 40-60/min.
Purkinje takeover — broad complex 15-30/min.

Broader QRS = lower escape focus = more haemodynamically unstable. In complete heart block, QRS width identifies escape focus.

*Explain the Frank-Starling mechanism and its relationship to excitation-contraction coupling.*

- **Q:** Frank-Starling law: state the law precisely (lab and clinical versions) and explain the 2 cellular mechanisms
  **A:** Law:
Lab: The force of contraction is proportional to the initial resting length of the myocardial muscle fibre
Clinical: Stroke volume increases with increasing EDV

2 cellular mechanisms:
1. Actin-myosin overlap — stretch toward optimal sarcomere length (~2.2 um) increases available crossbridge binding sites — more simultaneous crossbridge formation — greater force.

2. Ca2+ sensitivity — stretch increases sensitivity of troponin C to Ca2+ (via Titin) — same intracellular Ca2+ produces greater force. Dominant mechanism in physiological range.
- **Q:** Starling curve: describe the shape, effect of positive and negative inotropy, and why the descending limb is largely absent in the intact heart

Positive inotropy — curve shifts up and left (greater SV at same EDV)
Negative inotropy/failure — curve shifts down and right (less SV, compensatory EDV rise)

Descending limb: present in isolated muscle (sarcomere overstretch >2.2 um). Absent in intact heart — pericardium limits dilation and physiological EDVs stay within optimal sarcomere range.
- **Q:** Preload: define (lab and clinical) and list 5 determinants
  **A:** Definition:
Lab: sarcomere length at end diastole — initial resting fibre length just prior to contraction
Clinical: EDV (echo) or EDP — CVP approximates RVEDP, PCWP approximates LVEDP

1. Venous return (dominant)
VR = (MSFP − RAP) / resistance to venous return
  MSFP: blood volume, venomotor tone, venous compliance
  RAP: intrathoracic pressure, atrial function, pericardial pressure (↑RAP → ↓VR gradient)
  Resistance to VR: ↑IAP, obesity, pregnancy, posture, vascular compression

2. Heart rate — tachycardia → ↓filling time → ↓EDV

3. Atrial kick — contributes ~20% EDV; lost in AF

4. Ventricular compliance — stiff ventricle fills less at any given pressure

5. Pericardial compliance — directly limits diastolic expansion independent of venous return
(tamponade: ↑RAP + external compression — both mechanisms simultaneously)
- **Q:** Afterload: define and classify the determinants

Wall stress (Laplace):
\(\sigma = \frac{P \times r}{2h}\)
 P = transmural pressure (Ventricular cavity minus intrathoracic)
 r = ventricular radius — dilation ↑afterload
 h = wall thickness — hypertrophy ↓afterload

Most important factors affect Ventricular Transmural pressure

Intrathoracic: PPV -> ↑Intrathoracic Pressure = ↓tranmural pressure

Intra-cardiac
Increases: AS, PS, HOCM, SAM
Decreases: MR, TR (ejects into low-pressure atrium)

Extracardiac — Arterial input impedance:
SVR/PVR — (all things that impact R) Mainly vessel radius (Poiseuille), also viscosity, length is fixed
Arterial compliance — decreased compliance increases peak systolic pressure and speeds reflected waves arriving in systole

### 🟡 G3.iii `partial` · 2/~6 cards
*Define the components and determinants of cardiac output including the effects of positive pressure ventilation.*

- **Q:** Contractility: define and list 5 determinants
  **A:** Definition: The intrinsic ability of the myocardium to generate force at a given preload and afterload. Primarily determined by intracellular Ca2+ availability and myofilament sensitivity.

5 determinants:
1. Sympathetic tone — B1 — increased cAMP — PKA phosphorylates L-type Ca2+ channels — increased Ca2+ influx — increased SR loading — increased contractility

4. Drugs — positive: digoxin, catecholamines, milrinone. Negative: beta blockers, calcium channel blockers

---------------------
Imagine anRep. Afterload leads to a long contraction (rep), means more calcium
BowDitch- imagine a bow, firing fast (fast HR) salt crystal arrows (Na) into a ditch (the cell)
- **Q:** Contractility measurement: describe 3 methods and their limitations
  **A:** 1. dP/dt max — maximum rate of LV pressure rise during isovolumetric contraction. Greater rate = more forceful contraction.
Limitation: preload dependent. Requires LV catheterisation.

2. ESPVR — line through end-systolic points of multiple PV loops at varying preloads. Steeper slope = greater contractility.
Limitation: requires multiple PV loops. Invasive (research lab tool). Affected by afterload at extremes.

3. Ejection fraction — EF = SV/EDV x 100%, normal 55-75%. Most clinically used.
Limitation: load dependent — high afterload reduces EF without true change in contractility. Preserved EF does not exclude diastolic dysfunction.

Key principle: No measure of contractility is truly load-independent.

--------------------
Notes: for learning

dP/dt max

During isovolumetric contraction the mitral and aortic valves are both closed, so the ventricle is generating pressure without ejecting blood. The rate at which pressure rises in that phase reflects purely how fast and forcefully the myocytes can generate force — no confounding from ejection dynamics. You differentiate the LV pressure-time curve and find the peak — that's dP/dt max. Normal is around 1000-1200 mmHg/s.

The preload dependence is a problem because a more stretched ventricle (Frank-Starling) will also generate pressure faster — so a high dP/dt max might just mean high EDV, not high contractility.

ESPVR

At end-systole, the ventricle has finished ejecting and is at its smallest volume for that beat. If you plot multiple PV loops at different preloads (done by transiently reducing venous return — usually inferior vena cava occlusion with a balloon), the end-systolic points fall on a straight line. That line is the ESPVR, and its slope (Emax) reflects the stiffness of the ventricle at end-systole — which is a proxy for contractility.

The elegance is that by varying preload and finding where each loop terminates, you're isolating a property of the myocardium itself rather than a single load-dependent measurement. The limitation is practical — you need a pressure-volume catheter and a way to vary preload, so it's a research/cath lab tool. Afterload shifts the operating point along the line at extremes but doesn't change the slope much in normal ranges.

Ejection fraction

EF just measures how much of the EDV gets ejected. It's load-dependent in both directions — high afterload means the ventricle can't empty as well (reduced EF without reduced contractility), and low afterload flatters EF. The preserved EF point on your card is clinically important: in diastolic dysfunction the problem is the ventricle won't fill properly, so EDV is low and SV is low, but the ratio is maintained — EF looks normal while the patient has heart failure.

### ✅ G3.v `covered` · 6/~5 cards
*Describe and explain cardiac output curves, vascular function curves and their correlation.*

- **Q:** Cardiac output curve — axes, shape, and what shifts it up/down/right?
  **A:** Axes: x = RAP (atmospheric reference, mmHg); y = CO (L/min)
Shape: rises steeply (Frank-Starling), plateaus at maximum CO (~7.5 L/min normal)
Plateau: when transmural RAP ≤ 0 — great veins collapse, VR physically limited

↑ curve (↑CO for same RAP): ↑contractility, ↑HR, ↓afterload, ↓ITP (spontaneous inspiration)
↓ curve: ↓contractility (heart failure, septic cardiomyopathy), ↑afterload
Shift right: ↑ITP (PEEP) — same pump capability, but higher atmospheric RAP needed for same transmural filling
- **Q:** Vascular function curve — axes, shape, equation, and what shifts it?
  **A:** Axes: x = RAP (atmospheric, mmHg); y = venous return (L/min)
Equation: VR = (MSFP − RAP) / venous resistance
Shape: flat plateau at RAP ≤ 0 (great vein collapse), linear decline to x-intercept at MSFP

X-intercept = MSFP — VR = 0 when RAP = MSFP
Plateau = maximum VR — physical limit when great veins collapse

Shifts x-intercept right (↑MSFP): fluid, venoconstriction
Shifts x-intercept left (↓MSFP): haemorrhage, vasodilation
Steeper slope (↑resistance): ↓VR at any given RAP; pivot point = MSFP
- **Q:** Mean systemic filling pressure (MSFP) — definition, normal value, and determinants?
  **A:** Definition: the pressure that would equilibrate throughout the entire systemic vasculature if flow stopped — a static pressure; heart-independent
Normal: ~7–8 mmHg
Normal RAP: ~2 mmHg → venous return gradient = ~5–6 mmHg

Determinants:
1. Stressed blood volume
2. Venous tone

Venoconstriction → recruits unstressed → stressed volume → ↑MSFP without adding fluid
Haemorrhage → ↓stressed volume → ↓MSFP
- **Q:** Stressed vs unstressed blood volume — definitions, proportions, and clinical relevance?
  **A:** Unstressed volume: blood filling the vasculature without generating transmural pressure — occupying slack space; ~70% of total blood volume (~3.5L)
Stressed volume: blood above unstressed that stretches vessel walls and generates pressure; ~30% (~1.5L) — determines MSFP

Venoconstriction: reduces vessel capacity → recruits unstressed → stressed → ↑MSFP without adding fluid
Haemorrhage: loses stressed volume directly → ↓MSFP → ↓VR gradient
Vasodilation: ↑vessel capacity → stressed → unstressed → ↓MSFP
- **Q:** What does the intersection of the cardiac output and vascular function curves represent, and why must CO equal venous return at steady state?
  **A:** Intersection: the only combination of RAP and CO/VR satisfying both curves simultaneously — the actual operating point

Why CO = VR at steady state: the circulation is a closed loop in series — by conservation of flow, what leaves the heart must equal what returns. Any mismatch is transient and drives RAP up or down until equilibrium is restored

Neither curve alone predicts outcome — the cardiac curve says 'given this RAP, here's what the heart pumps'; the vascular curve says 'given this RAP, here's what the periphery delivers.' Reality is only the intersection.

- **Q:** Oxygen extraction ratio (OER) — equation, normal whole body value, normal myocardial value, and what happens below critical DO2?
  **A:** OER = VO2 / DO2 = (CaO2 − CvO2) / CaO2

Normal whole body at rest: ~25% (VO2 250 mL/min, DO2 1000 mL/min)

Below critical DO2: supply-dependent — VO2 falls with DO2; anaerobic metabolism begins
- **Q:** SvO2 — definition, sampling site, normal value, and causes of ↑ and ↓?

Sampled from: pulmonary artery (truly mixed)
Normal: 70–75%

From Fick: SvO2 = SaO2 − (VO2 / CO × Hb × 1.34)

↓SvO2: ↓CO, ↓SaO2, ↓Hb, ↑VO2 (fever, shivering, sepsis early)
↑SvO2: ↑CO (high output states), ↓VO2 (sedation, paralysis, hypothermia), cytotoxic hypoxia (cyanide), distributive shunting

*Describe the laws governing the behaviour of gases and liquid.*

- **Q:** Define the term Critical Point
  **A:** This is the point on a phase diagram where the liquid and gas phases of a substance have the same density, and are therefore indistinguishable.

This point is where a substance is at both its critical temp and critical pressure
- **Q:** Define the term Critical Pressure
  **A:** The minimum pressure required to liquify a vapour at its critical temperature
- **Q:** Define the term Critical Temperature
  **A:** This is the temperature above which a substance CANNOT be liquified, irrespective of how much pressure is applied.
- **Q:** Define the term Critical Volume.
  **A:** This is the volume occupied by a given amount of substance at its critical point.
- **Q:** Define the term freezing point.
  **A:** Freezing point is the temperature at which a substance changes state from liquid to solid, i.e. where liquid and solid phases coexist in equilibrium
- **Q:** What is Dalton's Law?
  **A:** "In a mixture of gases, the pressure exerted by each gas will be the same as if that gas alone were present"

The total pressure exerted by a mixture of gases is the sum of the partial pressure of the individual gases present

\[P_{\text{total}} = P_{\text{Gas 1}} + P_{\text{Gas 2}} + P_{\text{Gas 3}} + ...\]
A partial pressure is the pressure that a gas in a mixture of gases would exert if it were present alone under the same conditions.
- **Q:** Definition of a vapour:
  **A:** Gaseous form of a substance below its critical temperature
- **Q:** What is Boyle's Law?
  **A:** At a constant temperature, pressure and volume of a given mass of gas are inversely related\[P_{1}V_{1} = \text{constant} \]

Thus
\[P_{1}V_{1} = P_{2}V_{2}\]
- **Q:** Define the term "Latent heat of vapourisation"?
  **A:** AKA Massic enthalpy of evaporation

the heat required to convert 1g of a substance from the liquid phase to the gaseous phase at a given temperature (expressed in KJ.kg-1
- **Q:** What is the normal value, at room temp of 
Saturated water vapour pressure (SVP), and define the term
  **A:** the partial pressure exerted by water vapour when a gas is fully saturated with water at a given temperature—that is, when the rate of evaporation equals the rate of condensation. At this point, no additional water vapour can be taken up by the gas without condensation occurring.

 
At 37 degrees, this is 47mmHg
(Value is independent of atmospheric pressure)
- **Q:** Define the term "Water vapour pressure"?
  **A:** the partial pressure exerted by water vapour in any gas mixture
- **Q:** What is Charles' Law?
  **A:** At a constant pressure, the volume of a given mass of gas is directly proportional with the absolute temperature \[V \propto T \]or 
\[ \dfrac{V_1}{T_1} = \dfrac{V_2}{T_2} \]
- **Q:** What is meant by Critical temperature?

Give examples? O2, N2O?
  **A:** Temperature above which gas cannot be liquified by pressure aloneeg. O2 = -119oc, N2O = 36oc
- **Q:** Define a Dalton (unit of measurement)
  **A:** Unit of mass equal to 1/12th of the mass of a Carbon-12 atom (approx 1 proton)
- **Q:** Define Adiabatic?
  **A:** A process that occurs without transfer of heat or matter.

For example, gases heat up when compressed (greater than energy used to compress them) and cool when allowed to expand (adiabatic cooling)
- **Q:** Define Boiling Point?
  **A:** The temperature  at which the vapour pressure of a liquid equals the environmental pressure surrounding the liquid:- Therefore boiling point decreases as environmental pressure falls -> less external pressure keeping molecules in their liquid state
- Boiling differs from evaporation as molecules anywhere in the liquid may enter the gaseous phase, whilst evaporation only occurs at the surface
- **Q:** Define Density?
  **A:** Mass per unit volume
- **Q:** Define Dyne? (unit of Measurement)
  **A:** Force required to accelerate 1g by 1cm.sec-2
- **Q:** Define Flow?
  **A:** Quantity of fluid passing a point per unit time
- **Q:** Describe the Third Gas Law (Gay-Lussac)
  **A:** Pressure and temperature are linearly related when volume is constant

P = k x T

- **Q:** Define Impedance?
  **A:** Electrical context:Resistance to alternating current

Respiratory context:
Mechanical opposition to oscillating air flow. 
Incorporates airway resistance, and Reactance (which has elastic component, and inertial (non elastic component)
- **Q:** Define resonance in the context of an arterial line system
  **A:** Resonance: The phenomenon where a system oscillates with maximum amplitude when driven at its natural frequency, because energy is added in phase with the system's own oscillation.

In the arterial line context: The catheter-tubing-transducer system is a fluid-filled harmonic oscillator with its own natural frequency (typically 10–20 Hz for a standard system). The arterial waveform is not a pure sine wave — it contains a fundamental frequency (heart rate, ~1–2 Hz) plus harmonics at multiples of that frequency. When any of those harmonics approach the system's natural frequency, that component is disproportionately amplified → overshoot of the displayed waveform → falsely high systolic, falsely low diastolic, mean relatively preserved.
- **Q:** Define the natural frequency of an arterial line system
  **A:** The frequency at which the fluid-filled system oscillates freely when disturbed and left alone.
Typical clinical systems: 10–15 Hz.
Accurate BP measurement requires the natural frequency to be >>the highest signal harmonic — ideally >30 Hz.
- **Q:** 4 factors that lower the natural frequency of an arterial line system
  **A:** 1. Long tubing
2. Compliant (soft) tubing
3. Air bubbles (↑ compliance)
4. ↑ fluid mass in system
- **Q:** Overdamped vs underdamped arterial line — SBP, DBP, MAP?
  **A:** Underdamped: ↑SBP, ↓DBP
Overdamped: ↓SBP, ↑DBP
MAP: preserved in both
- **Q:** 4 causes of overdamping in an arterial line
  **A:** 1. Air bubble
2. Clot at cannula tip
3. Kinked cannula or tubing
4. Long, compliant, or narrow tubing

All increase energy dissipation or reduce transmission of the pressure wave.
- **Q:** Fast flush test — describe appearance for each: optimal, underdamped, overdamped
  **A:** Optimal (DC ~0.64): one overshoot + one undershoot → settles
Underdamped (DC <0.64): multiple oscillations, slow to settle
Overdamped (DC >1): no oscillations, sluggish return to baseline
